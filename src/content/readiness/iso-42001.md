## What is ISO/IEC 42001?

[ISO/IEC 42001:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001) is the first certifiable international standard for an artificial intelligence management system, abbreviated AIMS. It was published in December 2023 by ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, the joint committee responsible for AI standards. It specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving a management system for organizations that develop, provide or use AI systems.

The word "management system" matters. ISO/IEC 42001 does not specify how to secure a model or test a prompt. It specifies how an organization decides what to do, proves that it did it, and improves over time. Technical controls live in Annex A, and the organization selects which apply. That design is familiar to anyone who has worked with ISO/IEC 27001, and it is why a security team usually ends up owning a large share of the evidence.

## Who needs ISO 42001?

The standard applies to any organization that develops, provides or uses AI systems, regardless of size or sector. In practice three groups pursue it. Providers of AI products that sell into regulated industries pursue it because customers add it to procurement questionnaires. Organizations subject to the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) pursue it because a certified management system is strong evidence of the risk management and quality management obligations for high-risk systems, though it does not replace conformity assessment. And organizations already certified to ISO/IEC 27001 pursue it because the incremental cost is modest and the integrated audit is available from most certification bodies.

## How is the standard structured?

ISO/IEC 42001 follows the ISO harmonized structure, so clauses 4 to 10 will be recognizable to anyone who has implemented another ISO management system standard.

### Clause 4: context of the organization

The organization determines internal and external issues, the interested parties and their requirements, and the scope of the AIMS. Clause 4 also asks the organization to define its role: developer, provider, deployer or a combination. For MLSecOps this is the inventory step. An AIMS scope that says "all AI systems" without an inventory that can be regenerated will not survive stage 1.

### Clause 5: leadership

Top management must demonstrate commitment, establish an AI policy and assign roles, responsibilities and authorities. Annex A.2 (policies) and A.3 (internal organization) elaborate on the content of the policy and the roles. MLSecOpsHub recommends that the AI policy name the security risk classes explicitly: model supply chain, prompt injection and agent tooling. A policy that speaks only of ethics and fairness gives the security team nothing to anchor controls to.

### Clause 6: planning

Clause 6 is where ISO/IEC 42001 departs from its siblings. It requires two assessments. The AI risk assessment identifies risks to the organization from its AI systems. The AI system impact assessment identifies consequences for individuals, groups and society. Both feed a risk treatment plan and a Statement of Applicability that lists which Annex A controls are selected and why others are excluded. Annex A.5 specifies the impact assessment process in more detail, and Annex C provides a reference list of risk sources and objectives that the assessments can draw on.

For AI security work the risk assessment is a threat model. MLSecOpsHub uses MITRE ATLAS as the technique catalog and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications and OWASP ML Security Top 10 (v0.3 draft) as enumeration inputs, so that each identified risk carries an identifier that can be traced to a test.

### Clause 7: support

Resources, competence, awareness, communication and documented information. Annex A.4 elaborates on resources for AI systems: data, tooling, compute and human resources must be documented. Competence is where training records for engineers who handle model artifacts belong.

### Clause 8: operation

Operational planning and control, plus execution of the risk assessment, impact assessment and risk treatment at planned intervals and when significant changes occur. Clause 8 is short because the operational detail sits in Annex A.

### Clauses 9 and 10: performance evaluation and improvement

Monitoring and measurement, internal audit and management review in clause 9; nonconformity, corrective action and continual improvement in clause 10. Auditors read these clauses as evidence that the system is alive. An AIMS with no recorded nonconformities after a year of operation is treated with suspicion.

## What does Annex A contain?

Annex A lists control objectives and controls, grouped into nine areas, from A.2 to A.10. Annex B provides implementation guidance for each control. Annex D discusses applicability across domains and sectors. The organization selects controls in its Statement of Applicability; a control can be excluded, but the exclusion must be justified.

The areas, paraphrased:

- **A.2 Policies related to AI.** An AI policy, alignment with other policies, and periodic review.
- **A.3 Internal organization.** Roles, responsibilities and a process for reporting concerns.
- **A.4 Resources for AI systems.** Documentation of data, tooling, system and computing resources, and human resources.
- **A.5 Assessing impacts of AI systems.** The impact assessment process, its documentation, and consideration of individuals, groups and society.
- **A.6 AI system life cycle.** Objectives for responsible development, and controls for each lifecycle stage: requirements, design, verification and validation, deployment, operation and monitoring, technical documentation and event logging.
- **A.7 Data for AI systems.** Data management, acquisition, quality, provenance and preparation.
- **A.8 Information for interested parties.** System documentation, external reporting, incident communication and information for users.
- **A.9 Use of AI systems.** Processes for responsible use, objectives and intended use.
- **A.10 Third-party and customer relationships.** Allocation of responsibilities, supplier management and customer expectations.

## How do security controls satisfy Annex A?

Three areas carry most of the technical evidence: A.6, A.7 and A.10. MLSecOpsHub maps each to the three lanes of MLSecOps work.

### A.6 lifecycle controls and the model supply chain

The lifecycle controls in A.6.2 expect defined requirements, design, verification, deployment and operation stages, with records at each. A pipeline with security gates satisfies these directly. At intake, every model artifact is scanned for unsafe serialization and its signature verified. Python pickle files execute code on load; in February 2024 JFrog reported roughly one hundred malicious models on Hugging Face abusing exactly that mechanism. PyTorch 2.6 made `weights_only=True` the default for `torch.load`, and CVE-2025-32434 subsequently showed that the restricted loader could still be bypassed in affected versions. An A.6.2 verification record that shows an artifact was scanned and its loader version checked is concrete evidence; a policy sentence is not.

Before deployment, A.6.2.4 verification and validation is satisfied by adversarial test suites executed in CI: prompt-injection payloads against every template, authorization tests proving that injected instructions cannot reach tools outside scope, and resilience tests for poisoned retrieval sources. After deployment, A.6.2.6 operation and monitoring and A.6.2.8 event logging are satisfied by prompt and tool-call trace logging with a retention period that supports investigation.

### A.7 data controls and provenance

A.7 asks for documented data provenance, quality and preparation. The MLSecOps answer is an AI bill of materials generated in CI that lists base models, adapters, datasets, embedding services and, for agent systems, the MCP servers the agent connects to. For retrieval-augmented systems, A.7 also covers the corpus: ingestion pipelines should treat external documents as untrusted input, because indirect prompt injection arrives through data, not through the user. The incidents cataloged in the [Agentic Attack Index](/data) are mostly A.7 and A.9 failures viewed through a security lens.

### A.10 supplier controls and third-party models

A.10 requires that suppliers align with the organization's approach to AI and that responsibilities are allocated. Model providers, dataset publishers and MCP server publishers are suppliers. The intake procedure that satisfies A.6 at the artifact level satisfies A.10 at the relationship level: provenance and license checks, pinned digests, signature verification, and a supplier assessment record. Organizations that treat a public model hub as infrastructure rather than as a supplier typically have no A.10 evidence at all.

### A.9 and agent systems

A.9 asks for intended-use documentation and responsible-use processes. For agents, MLSecOpsHub reads this as a tool permission matrix scoped to least privilege and an approval gate for irreversible actions. An agent that can send email, modify a repository and read a shared credential store has an intended use that must be written down, and a set of permissions that must be justified against it.

## How does ISO 42001 relate to other standards?

ISO/IEC 42001 is designed to sit alongside ISO/IEC 27001. Both use the harmonized structure, so an organization can extend its existing information security management system with the AI-specific clauses and Annex A controls rather than build a parallel system. Most certification bodies offer integrated audits.

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on AI risk management and is the natural method for the clause 6 risk assessment. ISO/IEC 5338 describes the AI system life cycle and is referenced by Annex A.6. Neither is certifiable; both are useful for the "how" that ISO/IEC 42001 leaves open. The [NIST AI RMF](/readiness/nist-ai-rmf) is a further companion: NIST publishes a crosswalk between RMF subcategories and ISO/IEC 42001 clauses, and an organization that has implemented the RMF controls will have most of the technical evidence the AIMS needs.

## What does the certification path look like?

Certification is issued by an accredited certification body, not by ISO. The path has four steps.

1. **Readiness.** Build the AIMS, run at least one internal audit and one management review, and collect evidence for a period long enough to show operation.
2. **Stage 1 audit.** A document review: scope, policy, risk assessment, impact assessment, Statement of Applicability and the mandatory records. The auditor confirms the organization is ready for stage 2.
3. **Stage 2 audit.** An implementation audit: the auditor samples AI systems in scope, interviews owners, inspects pipeline evidence and tests whether controls operate as described.
4. **Surveillance and recertification.** Annual surveillance audits during a three-year cycle, then a recertification audit.

The technical evidence that the security team owns is sampled in stage 2. Auditors ask to see the pipeline definition, a recent scan result, a recent test run, the AI-BOM for a named system, an alert that fired, and the corrective action that followed. If these are in a build system with history, stage 2 is short.

## Where do organizations usually fall short?

MLSecOpsHub records three recurring gaps. First, the Statement of Applicability selects A.6 and A.7 controls but points to no mechanism; the auditor asks what implements A.6.2.4 and receives a process description rather than a CI job. Second, third-party models and MCP servers are not treated as suppliers, so A.10 has no evidence. Third, the impact assessment is completed once and never revisited, which fails clause 8's requirement to reassess on significant change; a new tool added to an agent is a significant change.
